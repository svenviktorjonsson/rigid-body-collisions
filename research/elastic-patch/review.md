# Mechanics review: elastic contact with an independent torque impulse

Prepared 2026-10-05. This review separates a physically admissible contact
representation, a verified numerical example and experimental material
validation. It does not claim that the current engine already implements
every law discussed here.

## Finding

A contact can be represented computationally by a linear impulse and an
independent angular impulse applied at one location. The latter is a free
couple; it is not restricted to the moment generated by the linear impulse.
This permits an elastic reversal of spin about the surface normal, which a
point force at the bottom of a spherical ball cannot produce. A physical
interpretation requires a finite effective contact patch, deformation,
bonding or another identified mechanism. Concentrating its resultant wrench
at one computational point does not remove its material length scale.

Static friction bounds the traction that can be transmitted without plastic
slip. Elastic shear and twisting deformation store the energy that can
subsequently reverse motion. Coulomb friction alone dissipates energy and
cannot reverse an initially isolated spin by elastically returning energy.
Large friction capacity permits an elastic rebound; it does not determine
the rebound without stiffness, damping and contact duration.

The proposed distinction is sound, but the contact algebra, elastic
tangential/torsional compliance and normal-load-dependent friction moments
are established mechanics. A publishable contribution would require a
demonstrated improvement in accuracy, cost or a reproducible adaptive
strategy, rather than claiming these ingredients themselves as new.

## Wrench and work

For contact impulses on body A, use linear impulse $p$ and independent angular
impulse $L$; body B receives their negatives. With each $r$ measured from its
own center of mass to the contact location,

$$
\Delta p_A=p,\qquad \Delta L_A=r_A\wedge p+L,
\qquad
\Delta p_B=-p,\qquad \Delta L_B=-r_B\wedge p-L.
$$

The wedge is the standard cross-product matrix: r wedge p means r cross p.
The contact relative velocities are

$$
u=v_A+\omega_A\times r_A-v_B-\omega_B\times r_B,
\qquad s=\omega_A-\omega_B.
$$

The corresponding instantaneous contact power is

$$
\mathcal P=u\cdot F+s\cdot\tau.
$$

Stacking both channels preserves their coupling. For a frozen contact map,
the relative velocity change is K times the stacked impulse, where K is the
full symmetric contact mobility, not six independent scalar mobilities.
The kinetic-energy change is

$$
\Delta E_{\rm kin}
=\begin{bmatrix}u^-\\s^-\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T}
  \begin{bmatrix}p\\L\end{bmatrix}
+\frac12\begin{bmatrix}p\\L\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T}
  K\begin{bmatrix}p\\L\end{bmatrix}.
$$

These mixed physical units are consistent in the work pairing. A temporary
representation length can scale the angular channel, but cannot be used as
the material patch radius. For arbitrary shapes, full inertia and
off-diagonal mobility coupling must remain in the energy calculation.

Assigning separate restitution values to every channel without checking
this quadratic can create energy. Accepted isolated collisions must satisfy
kinetic plus elastic energy after, plus accumulated dissipation, equals
initial kinetic plus elastic energy, within numerical tolerance. Prescribed
walls add their measured external work to the right-hand side.
For a model with elastic coupling between normal compression and twist,
energy can transfer between those channels. A measured spin rebound ratio
greater than one can therefore be passive if normal kinetic energy decreases;
likewise a normal rebound ratio greater than one can use initial spin energy.
Bounds on one channel must not replace the total energy balance.

## Normal-force-dependent torsion and shared friction capacity

Let $N$ be compressive normal force and $a$ the effective circular patch radius.
For a normal-axis torsional moment, the elementary bound is

$$
|\tau_n|\leq\mu_{\rm spin}aN.
$$

The coefficient and radius are physical parameters. For a specified
nonnegative pressure $p(r)$, local Coulomb traction gives the sharper fully
sliding pure-torsion capacity

$$
|\tau_n|_{\max}=\mu\int_{\rm patch}r\,p(r)\,dA.
$$

Direct integration gives $2\mu aN/3$ for uniform circular pressure, and
$3\pi\mu aN/16$ for circular Hertz pressure. These two factors are derived
here from their respective pressure distributions, not fitted data. They
are maximum pure-torsion capacities, not a claim that partial-slip response
is linear up to the bound.

If $a$ is fixed during contact, the corresponding integrated angular-impulse
bound is $|L_n|\leq\mu_{\rm spin}aP_n$. A time-varying patch requires
integration of $\mu_{\rm spin}a(t)N(t)$, rather than substituting the final radius.
Meeting an endpoint impulse bound is necessary; it does not prove that the
instantaneous force/moment limits were respected throughout an impact.

Sliding and twisting use the same traction field. Giving each its entire
pure-channel capacity independently can overestimate what one patch can
transmit. For symmetric pressure, a conservative realizable inner bound is

$$
\frac{\|F_t\|}{\mu N}
+\frac{|\tau_n|}{c\mu aN}\leq1,
\quad c=\frac23\text{ (uniform)},\quad
c=\frac{3\pi}{16}\text{ (Hertz)}.
$$

Its justification is a convex combination of admissible pure-sliding and
pure-twisting traction fields; it is not the exact patch yield surface.
An ellipsoidal combined force/twist limit is another established engineering
approximation, supported by soft-finger contact literature [Xydas and Kao,
1999; Howe and Cutkosky, 1996, below]. Its shape and capacities should be
derived from a pressure/traction model or calibrated; an ellipsoid is not
automatically an inner bound for every pressure distribution.
Zero normal force gives zero unbonded traction capacity. Rolling moments
about tangent axes are distinct from normal-axis twisting and require an
appropriate pressure/deformation mechanism rather than reusing the torsion
formula indiscriminately. In 2D only the out-of-plane angular channel exists;
the full 3D twisting-versus-rolling distinction cannot be tested in 2D.

## Exact reversal examples suitable for verification

### Spin about the surface normal

Take a spherical body of mass $m$ and normal-axis inertia $I_n$, contacting a
fixed horizontal plane with approach speed $V$, spin $\Omega_0$, a linear normal
spring $k_n$, a torsion spring $k_\theta$, and zero damping. For this aligned
spherical case the normal and torsion channels decouple. Choose

$$
k_\theta=\frac{I_n k_n}{m},\qquad
\nu=\sqrt{\frac{k_n}{m}},\qquad T=\frac{\pi}{\nu}.
$$

Before any yield, normal compression and elastic twist are

$$
\delta(t)=\frac{V}{\nu}\sin(\nu t),\qquad
\theta(t)=\frac{\Omega_0}{\nu}\sin(\nu t).
$$

Both springs return to zero energy at separation $T$. Normal velocity becomes
$+V$ and spin becomes $-\Omega_0$. Throughout the contact the torque-to-normal-
force ratio is $I_n|\Omega_0|/(mV)$. Therefore an exact
no-yield sufficient condition in this isolated pure-torsion example is

$$
\kappa_{\rm static}\geq\frac{I_n|\Omega_0|}{mV},
\qquad |\tau_n|\leq\kappa_{\rm static}N.
$$

For a uniform solid sphere, $I_n=2mR^2/5$. This is an analytically
verified synthetic example, not a measured rubber constitutive law. A small
patch and ordinary friction may fail this capacity condition at high spin.
It would be misleading to raise friction or patch radius silently to make a
particular observed ball reverse.

### Spin about an axis parallel to the surface

Tangential traction can change this spin through r cross p even without an
independent torque. Define $\alpha=I/(mR^2)$, take initial horizontal
center-of-mass velocity zero, and select signs such that the contact slip is
$u_t=v_t-R\Omega$. If an elastic tangential response gives
$u_t^+=-e_tu_t^-$, its exact isolated endpoint is

$$
\Omega^+=\frac{\alpha-e_t}{1+\alpha}\Omega^-,\qquad
v_t^+=\frac{(1+e_t)\alpha}{1+\alpha}R\Omega^-.
$$

For a solid sphere $\alpha=2/5$ and $e_t=1$, spin becomes $-3\Omega/7$ while
horizontal center-of-mass velocity becomes $4R\Omega/7$. This exchanges
rotational and translational energy. Final inelastic sticking $e_t=0$ leaves
spin $+2\Omega/7$ and does not reverse it. Reversal here requires $e_t>\alpha$
and sufficient friction capacity during the elastic episode.

### Floor, ceiling and backward bounces

Selected elastic parameters and initial conditions can produce backward
bounces or a periodic floor-ceiling trajectory. A general spinning throw
does not retrace its path: changing contact normal, tangential impulse,
spin, gravity, damping and contact duration all affect the next incidence.
Test one analytically constructed reversible orbit as an existence example,
and perturb speed, spin and friction to expose its limits. Do not claim
unconditional retroreflection or that every rubber ball reverses spin.

## Evidence needed before material or full-engine claims

1. Compare isolated torsional and tangential episodes against the exact
   examples above, including normal impulse, angular impulse, spring-energy
   peak, zero residual spring energy for the elastic example, energy/work
   balance and instantaneous yield bounds.
2. Include low friction and dissipative baselines that do not reverse.
   With sliding, accumulated plastic dissipation must be nonnegative.
3. Keep contact memory through compression and release. Disclose residual
   elastic energy at separation; do not erase it without accounting for
   internal vibration, returned energy or explicit dissipation.
4. Refine time/event resolution independently. Compare observed endpoint
   restitution to prescribed stiffness/damping, rather than declaring them
   identical by construction.
5. For a real rubber ball, use measured mass, inertia, geometry, normal
   restitution versus impact speed, elastic tangential/torsional response,
   surface friction and contact duration or patch size. The references below
   establish plausibility, not those parameters for this engine.
6. Separately integrate the law into changing 3D manifolds and simultaneous
   contacts. A frozen-contact spin demonstration is not validation of a
   shaking container, a multi-contact restitution rule or collision
   discovery.

## References and verification scope

The following titles, authors, publication dates and DOI records were checked
against Crossref on 2026-10-05. Unless a full-text read is specified below,
abstract claims come from publisher-supplied abstracts returned by Crossref;
paywalled full derivations were not audited. Cross's 2005 public full text and
University of Sydney bouncing-ball page were read. The measurements quoted
below are published aggregate data, not original experimental work or an
engine fit.

- **R. D. Mindlin (1949), “Compliance of Elastic Bodies in Contact,” Journal
  of Applied Mechanics 16, 259-268.**
  <https://doi.org/10.1115/1.4009973>. The abstract explicitly considers both
  a small tangential force and a small torsional couple across an elliptic
  elastic contact surface, gives tangential/torsional compliance, and notes
  edge slip. Direct prior art for elastic torque at a finite patch.
- **R. D. Mindlin and H. Deresiewicz (1953), “Elastic Spheres in Contact Under
  Varying Oblique Forces,” Journal of Applied Mechanics 20, 327-344.**
  <https://doi.org/10.1115/1.4010702>. The abstract states that slip,
  dissipation and permanent set make response depend on the full loading
  history and instantaneous normal/tangential loading rates. Supports
  maintaining contact history; it does not validate a chosen lumped spring.
- **N. Maw, J. R. Barber and J. N. Fawcett (1981), “The Role of Elastic
  Tangential Compliance in Oblique Impact,” Journal of Lubrication
  Technology 103, 74-80.** <https://doi.org/10.1115/1.3251617>. Publisher
  abstract reports significant rebound-angle changes near the friction
  angle and agreement with steel and rubber experiments. Their earlier
  1976 paper, “The oblique impact of elastic spheres,” is
  <https://doi.org/10.1016/0043-1648(76)90201-5>; its metadata was checked.
- **Rod Cross (2005), “Bounce of a spinning ball near normal incidence,”
  American Journal of Physics 73, 914-920.**
  <https://doi.org/10.1119/1.2008299>. Abstract states that a spinning ball
  incident normally bounces obliquely with reduced spin, and reports normal
  and tangential restitution for a tennis ball on smooth/rough surfaces and
  racquet strings. This contradicts a universal retracing-path claim.
  **Public full text read:**
  <https://www.physics.usyd.edu.au/~cross/PUBLICATIONS/31.%20Spin.pdf>.
  Equations (3)-(4) confirm the tangential-only endpoints derived above.
  For nearly vertical spinning tennis-ball impacts, Table I reports:

  | Surface | Normal restitution | Tangential restitution | Spin after/before |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | Smooth wood | 0.77 ± 0.008 | 0.08 ± 0.04 | 0.29 ± 0.03 |
  | P800 abrasive on wood | 0.78 ± 0.011 | 0.055 ± 0.04 | 0.30 ± 0.04 |

  Nine bounces were averaged for each case; the reported errors are standard
  deviations, not numerical tolerances. Initial speeds were 6.09 ± 0.44 and
  5.93 ± 0.46 m/s respectively, with initial spin ranges -61 to -116 and -65
  to -90 rad/s. The spin ratios appear in the accompanying results text.
  Section IV cites sliding friction 0.4 ± 0.1 for smooth wood and
  0.85 ± 0.05 for P800. Despite greater friction, the tennis ball still did
  not reverse its spin. The paper uses radius 33 mm and inertia ratio
  $\alpha=0.55$ for the tennis ball. These are tangential-axis spinning
  impacts, not normal-axis torsional-spin measurements. Averaged outcomes
  cannot reconstruct individual trials; an eventual material-validation
  comparison needs compatible initial-condition ranges and uncertainty.
- **Rod Cross (2018), “Backward bounce of a spinning ball,” European Journal
  of Physics 39, 045007.** <https://doi.org/10.1088/1361-6404/aabece>.
  Abstract explains that a backspinning ball near normal incidence can
  bounce backwards, including sequences with forward bounces before a
  backward bounce. Supports the existence of backward rebound, not universal
  spin reversal or an exact floor-ceiling periodic orbit.
- **Rod Cross (2021), “Vertical bounce of a spinning ball,” Physics Education
  56, 023002.** <https://doi.org/10.1088/1361-6552/abd0d8>. Abstract describes
  video experiments with three vertically dropped spinning balls and
  theoretical estimates of their changed spin and horizontal speed.
- **Rod Cross, public “Physics of Bouncing Balls and Other Objects.”**
  <https://www.physics.usyd.edu.au/~cross/BOUNCE.htm>. Read on 2026-10-05.
  The page describes elastic tangential restitution and video of a hollow
  rubber ball on granite. This is qualitative accessible experimental
  context, not a data set already calibrated in this project.
- **N. Xydas and I. Kao (1999), “Modeling of Contact Mechanics and Friction
  Limit Surfaces for Soft Fingers in Robotics, with Experimental Results,”
  International Journal of Robotics Research.**
  <https://doi.org/10.1177/02783649922066673>. Publisher abstract read; full
  derivation not audited. It reports rubber/silicone contact experiments,
  normal-force-dependent patch radius, and approximation of numerical
  force/twist friction limit surfaces by an ellipse. Relevant evidence for
  a lumped soft-contact wrench and a source of prior art; these static
  manipulation experiments do not calibrate an impact model for this ball.
- **R. D. Howe and M. R. Cutkosky (1996), “Practical Force-Motion Models for
  Sliding Manipulation,” International Journal of Robotics Research.**
  <https://doi.org/10.1177/027836499601500603>. Publisher abstract read; full
  derivation not audited. It compares force/motion approximations including
  ellipsoids, cones and pyramids against experiments, and distinguishes
  computational speed, accuracy and conservatism. Supports an explicitly
  approximate engineering limit surface rather than an exact universal law.
- **W. J. Stronge (1990), “Rigid body collisions with friction,” Proceedings
  of the Royal Society A 431, 169-181.**
  <https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1990.0125>. Publisher abstract was also read
  directly during this review. It addresses energy consistency
  and slip reversal; independently assigned restitution is not automatically
  energy-admissible for coupled frictional impacts.

The defensible consensus is therefore: implement and test the independent
elastic torque channel, label synthetic demonstrations honestly, and pursue
publishability through measured solver improvements and material validation.
The existing mechanics alone are not a new collision theory.
