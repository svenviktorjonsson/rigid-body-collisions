\begin{quote}\textbf{Model fidelity correction (6 October 2026).} The historical force-only predictions and comparisons in this draft do not validate the requested motion-directed linear and angular impulse model. Its direction $t$ follows relative contact velocity, and $s$ follows relative angular velocity; these are not an arbitrary tangent basis. \begin{abstract}
A fixed reference length places linear and angular motion in one dimensionally consistent vector. Its dual places linear and angular momentum in one vector without changing power, kinetic energy, contact response, or physical predictions. We derive shared-point contact, simultaneous impulses, normal and tangential restitution, and a Coulomb capacity constraint. Public rubber-ball and limestone experiments provide partial agreement, not independent validation of all material parameters. Geometric uncertainty and a remaining rubber spin discrepancy are retained explicitly.
\end{abstract}

\section{The reference length and the dual variables}
Let $\Lref>0$ be a fixed reference length, $\mathbf v$ the centre-of-mass velocity, $\boldsymbol\omega$ the angular velocity, $\mathbf p=m\mathbf v$ the linear momentum, and $\mathbf L=\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega$ the angular momentum about the centre of mass. All spatial vectors and the inertia tensor use the same world frame. Define
\begin{equation}
\mathbf V=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf v\\ \Lref\boldsymbol\omega\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf P=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf p\\\mathbf L/\Lref\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf M=\begin{bmatrix}m\mathbf1&0\\0&\mathbf I/\Lref^2\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Then $\mathbf P=\mathbf M\mathbf V$. Every component of $\mathbf V$ has units m/s, every component of $\mathbf P$ has units kg m/s, and every block of $\mathbf M$ has units kg. The dual pairing is unchanged:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf V^T\mathbf P=\mathbf v^T\mathbf p+\boldsymbol\omega^T\mathbf L,
\qquad T=\tfrac12\mathbf V^T\mathbf M\mathbf V.
\end{equation}
For force $\mathbf f$ and centre-of-mass torque $\boldsymbol\tau$, the corresponding scaled wrench is $\mathbf F=[\mathbf f^T,\boldsymbol\tau^T/\Lref]^T$ and power is $\mathbf V^T\mathbf F$. This is a coordinate choice, \emph{not} a friction, restitution, or material-fit parameter. A different fixed length per body is allowed; use that body's length in each block. Changing the length during an integration step requires additional coordinate-derivative terms and is excluded here.

\section{Relation to established spatial notation}
Six-component vectors combining translation and rotation, and their dual force/momentum vectors, are established spatial-vector and screw-theory constructions. Featherstone describes the different transformation rules for motion and force vectors; Modern Robotics derives their power pairing\cite{featherstone,modernrobotics}. Characteristic-length normalization also has prior use in robotics to make mixed translational/rotational Jacobians dimensionally homogeneous\cite{angeles}.

A particularly close precedent is Vose, Umbanhowar and Lynch (2011): their appendix footnote 1 defines a planar velocity twist with angular velocity multiplied by radius of gyration and a force wrench with torque divided by that radius\cite{vose}. Zhang, Su and Liao (2014), section 3.3, equation 21, likewise scale rotational deformation by a characteristic length and applied moment by its reciprocal\cite{zhang}. These are explicit dual length scalings, rather than merely six-component notation. For fixed length, the momentum scaling follows by integrating the scaled wrench over an impulse. We have not established priority for applying this pairing to momentum.

Our choices are translation first, the fixed scale $\ell$, the dual angular momentum $\mathbf L/\ell$, and the signed lever operator that keeps the contact-velocity formula additive. To see precisely what is being changed, define the unscaled centre-of-mass vectors and scale matrix
\begin{equation}
\mathbf w=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf v\\\boldsymbol\omega\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf h=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf p\\\mathbf L\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf D=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf1_3,\ell\mathbf1_3),\qquad
\mathbf M_0=\operatorname{diag}(m\mathbf1_3,\mathbf I).
\end{equation}
Then the entire formulation follows from an invertible coordinate change and its dual:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf V=\mathbf D\mathbf w,\qquad
\mathbf P=\mathbf D^{-T}\mathbf h,\qquad
\mathbf M=\mathbf D^{-T}\mathbf M_0\mathbf D^{-1},\qquad
\mathbf V^T\mathbf P=\mathbf w^T\mathbf h.
\end{equation}
For the unscaled contact map $\mathbf C_0=[\mathbf1_3\ {-\crossmat{\mathbf r}}]$, the scaled map is $\mathbf C=\mathbf C_0\mathbf D^{-1}$. Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf C\mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf C^T=\mathbf C_0\mathbf M_0^{-1}\mathbf C_0^T.
\end{equation}
The dual momentum scaling is therefore required by the chosen velocity scaling if work and energy are to be retained. The contact response is unchanged. Keeping plus signs follows from cross-product antisymmetry; omitting redundant parentheses follows from matrix associativity. Neither operation is a new algebraic rule.

The contribution of this presentation is to carry these choices consistently through 2D/3D contact, restitution, friction capacity and efficient matrix evaluation. We do not claim a new physical law, new spatial-vector mathematics, or priority for this exact notation. The sources establish substantial prior art; the present review is not an exhaustive priority search.

\section{Contact velocity with plus signs}
Write $\crossmat{\mathbf a}\mathbf b=\mathbf a\times\mathbf b$. The skew matrix obeys $\crossmat{\mathbf a}^T=-\crossmat{\mathbf a}$. For the physical contact lever arm $\mathbf r=\mathbf x_c-\mathbf x_{\rm COM}$, set $\boldsymbol\rho=\mathbf r/\Lref$ and define the \emph{signed lever operator}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf R=\crossmat{-\boldsymbol\rho}.
\end{equation}
The physical velocity at the contact is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf v_c=\mathbf v+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r
=\mathbf v+\mathbf R\Lref\boldsymbol\omega
=\mathbf C\mathbf V,
\qquad \mathbf C=[\mathbf1\ \mathbf R].
\end{equation}
The plus sign is obtained by reversing the cross-product order, since $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r=(-\mathbf r)\times\boldsymbol\omega$. It does \emph{not} mean ordinary matrix factors commute. Associativity allows $\mathbf R\Lref\boldsymbol\omega$ to be written without redundant parentheses, but does not allow $\mathbf A\mathbf B$ to be replaced by $\mathbf B\mathbf A$.

A general contact transfers both a force impulse $\mathbf j=\int\mathbf f_c\,dt$ and a torque impulse $\mathbf k=\int\boldsymbol\tau_c\,dt$. Here $\boldsymbol\tau_c$ is the free contact moment about the declared contact reference point, excluding the lever-arm moment already represented by $\mathbf r\times\mathbf f_c$. The complete momentum update is
\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathbf p=\mathbf j,\qquad
\boxed{\Delta\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf j+\mathbf k},\qquad
\Delta\mathbf P=\mathbf C^T\mathbf j+
\begin{bmatrix}0\\\mathbf k/\ell\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
For a frozen contact lever arm, a distributed traction impulse over a contact patch $\mathcal A$ produces
\begin{equation}
\mathbf j=\int\!\int_{\mathcal A}\mathbf t(\mathbf x,t)\,dA\,dt,\qquad
\mathbf k=\int\!\int_{\mathcal A}(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_c)\times\mathbf t(\mathbf x,t)\,dA\,dt.
\end{equation}
An independently applied couple is included in $\mathbf k$ as well. This decomposition accounts for the same physical wrench exactly once. If the reference point shifts by $\mathbf a$, then $\mathbf r'=\mathbf r+\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf k'=\mathbf k-\mathbf a\times\mathbf j$, so $\mathbf r'\times\mathbf j+\mathbf k'=\mathbf r\times\mathbf j+\mathbf k$.

Choose a fixed contact length $\ell_c>0$ and define the scaled contact wrench impulse and conjugate contact motion
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\eta=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf j\\\mathbf k/\ell_c\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf U_c=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf v_c\\\ell_c\boldsymbol\omega\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf K=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf1&\mathbf R\\0&(\ell_c/\ell)\mathbf1\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Then the additive form carries the independent torque impulse without a separate update rule:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf U_c=\mathbf K\mathbf V,\qquad
\Delta\mathbf P=\mathbf K^T\boldsymbol\eta
=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf j\\\boldsymbol\rho\times\mathbf j+\mathbf k/\ell\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Work duality gives
\begin{equation}
\mathbf V^T\Delta\mathbf P=\mathbf v_c^T\mathbf j+\boldsymbol\omega^T\mathbf k
=\mathbf U_c^T\boldsymbol\eta.
\end{equation}
Taking $\ell_c=\ell$ makes the lower-right block of $\mathbf K$ an identity. The ideal force-only point-contact specialization sets $\mathbf k=0$ and retains just the three rows $\mathbf C$; it cannot represent a finite-patch free torque impulse. Contact wrench/moment models and torsional/rolling friction have established precedents\cite{drakewrench,mujocowrench}.

For planar motion, $\omega$, $L$, and $I$ are scalars, while translation is two-dimensional. The same construction becomes
\begin{equation}
\mathbf V=\begin{bmatrix}v_x\\v_y\\\ell\omega\end{bmatrix},\quad
\mathbf P=\begin{bmatrix}p_x\\p_y\\L/\ell\end{bmatrix},\quad
\mathbf M=\operatorname{diag}(m,m,I/\ell^2),\quad
\mathbf R=\frac1\ell\begin{bmatrix}-r_y\\r_x\end{bmatrix},\quad
\mathbf C=[\mathbf1_2\ \mathbf R].
\end{equation}
Thus $\mathbf C\mathbf V=\mathbf v+\omega(-r_y,r_x)^T$. Include the scalar free torque impulse $k$ through
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\eta=\begin{bmatrix}j_x\\j_y\\k/\ell_c\end{bmatrix},\quad
\mathbf K=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&-r_y/\ell\\0&1&r_x/\ell\\0&0&\ell_c/\ell\end{bmatrix},\quad
\Delta L=r_xj_y-r_yj_x+k.
\end{equation}
The same transpose and duality construction therefore carries over without inventing a planar angular vector.

\section{Two bodies and simultaneous contact}
Orient a contact normal from body B towards body A. Transfer $+\mathbf j,+\mathbf k$ to A and $-\mathbf j,-\mathbf k$ to B, with the force impulse applied at the same world reference point. Use the same contact scale $\ell_c$ for the two contact maps, even if their body scales differ. The relative contact motion and stacked Jacobian are
\begin{equation}
\mathbf U=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf u\\\ell_c\boldsymbol\Omega\end{bmatrix}
=\mathbf K_A\mathbf V_A-\mathbf K_B\mathbf V_B
=\mathbf G_w\mathbf V,\qquad
\mathbf G_w=[\mathbf K_A\ {-\mathbf K_B}],
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf u=\mathbf v_{c,A}-\mathbf v_{c,B}$ and $\boldsymbol\Omega=\boldsymbol\omega_A-\boldsymbol\omega_B$. For $\mathbf M=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf M_A,\mathbf M_B)$,
\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathbf P=\mathbf G_w^T\boldsymbol\eta,\qquad
\mathbf V^+=\mathbf V^-+\mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf G_w^T\boldsymbol\eta,\qquad
\mathbf U^+=\mathbf U^-+\mathbf W_w\boldsymbol\eta,
\end{equation}
with the full contact-wrench inverse effective mass
\begin{equation}
\mathbf W_w=\mathbf G_w\mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf G_w^T
=\mathbf K_A\mathbf M_A^{-1}\mathbf K_A^T
+\mathbf K_B\mathbf M_B^{-1}\mathbf K_B^T.
\end{equation}
This symmetric positive-semidefinite operator couples force and torque impulses, translation and relative spin, and all contacts sharing a body. A fixed boundary contributes its prescribed contact velocity and angular velocity but no finite inverse mass. Stack every contact's active force and moment components; do not discard off-diagonal coupling in rapid group motion.

For the force-only specialization $\mathbf k=0$, the first three rows of each $\mathbf K$ are $\mathbf C$, giving $\mathbf G=[\mathbf C_A\ {-\mathbf C_B}]$ and $\mathbf W=\mathbf G\mathbf M^{-1}\mathbf G^T$. The physical predictions are independent of the chosen body and contact coordinate lengths when impulses are transformed consistently.

For a frozen configuration during an impulse, the exact kinetic-energy increment for two dynamic bodies, or one body and a stationary boundary, is
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\Delta T=\boldsymbol\eta^T\mathbf U^-+\tfrac12\boldsymbol\eta^T\mathbf W_w\boldsymbol\eta}.
\end{equation}
The linear work term is explicitly $\mathbf j^T\mathbf u^-+\mathbf k^T\boldsymbol\Omega^-$. This retains torque-impulse work, including pure relative spin. For a moving prescribed boundary,
\begin{equation}
W_{\rm actuator}=\mathbf j^T\mathbf v_b(\mathbf x_c)+\mathbf k^T\boldsymbol\omega_b,\qquad
\Delta T-W_{\rm actuator}
=\boldsymbol\eta^T\mathbf U_{\rm relative}^-+\tfrac12\boldsymbol\eta^T\mathbf W_w\boldsymbol\eta.
\end{equation}
For multiple contacts, use stacked vectors and the full operator. Free flight still uses the actual rigid-body evolution, changing world inertia and gyroscopic dynamics; the impulsive relation does not replace that evolution.

\subsection{Pure spin with a central normal impact}
For a sphere on a plane with $\mathbf r=-R\mathbf n$, a central normal force impulse $\mathbf j=j_n\mathbf n$ has $\mathbf r\times\mathbf j=0$. If the sphere spins about $\mathbf n$, then $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r=0$ as well. An ideal force-only point contact consequently cannot change this spin. A nonzero torsional contact impulse gives
\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathbf L=\mathbf k,\qquad
\boldsymbol\omega^+=\boldsymbol\omega^-+\mathbf I^{-1}\mathbf k.
\end{equation}
For spin perpendicular to $\mathbf n$, $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r$ generally produces contact slip, so a tangential force impulse can also change spin through $\mathbf r\times\mathbf j_t$. For a non-spherical off-centre contact even a normal impulse may have a nonzero lever-arm moment. The complete wrench formulation includes each mechanism.

\section{Normal and tangential restitution}
Choose an orthonormal basis $\mathbf Q=[\mathbf n\ \mathbf t_1\ \mathbf t_2]$ and write $\widehat{\mathbf u}=\mathbf Q^T\mathbf u$, $\widehat{\mathbf j}=\mathbf Q^T\mathbf j$. For an approaching geometrically active impact, the endpoint targets are
\begin{equation}
u_n^+=-e_nu_n^-,\qquad
\mathbf u_t^+=-e_t\mathbf u_t^-,\qquad
0\le e_n\le1,\quad -1\le e_t\le1.
\end{equation}
The signed tangential convention matters: $e_t=-1$ preserves tangential contact velocity, $e_t=0$ targets sticking, and $e_t>0$ targets slip reversal. For $-1<e_t<0$, tangential contact motion continues in its original direction with reduced magnitude. For example, $e_t=-.2$ gives $\mathbf u_t^+=.2\mathbf u_t^-$: this sign describes the direction of a velocity ratio, not negative elastic stiffness or energy creation. Normal and tangential restitution describe different responses and need not be equal. These are \emph{contact-point} velocities; a centre-of-mass tangential speed ratio is not generally $e_t$. In coupled geometries, actual total energy must still be checked.

Set $\mathbf E=\operatorname{diag}(e_n,e_t,e_t)$. In the force-only specialization $\mathbf k=0$, if the isolated full-rank contact can realize all targets, its impulse solves
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathbf W}\widehat{\mathbf j}
+(\mathbf1+\mathbf E)\widehat{\mathbf u}^-=0,
\qquad \widehat{\mathbf W}=\mathbf Q^T\mathbf W\mathbf Q.
\end{equation}
With an independent torque impulse the translational endpoint instead reads
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathbf W}_{ff}\widehat{\mathbf j}
+\widehat{\mathbf W}_{fk}\widehat{\mathbf k}/\ell_c
+(\mathbf1+\mathbf E)\widehat{\mathbf u}^-=0.
\end{equation}
Here the blocks are taken from $\mathbf W_w$ after rotating both its force and moment components into the contact basis. Thus the three translational restitution targets do not determine a six-component contact wrench. Its moment components require a separately specified physical contact law or measured torque impulse. Any rotational restitution targets must likewise be independently justified, with actual coupled energy and friction limits checked.

This residual form keeps addition visible without concealing physical signs. In a contact network, unilateral conditions, friction capacity, and coupled energy constraints may prevent all endpoint targets being realized simultaneously. Bounds on individual coefficients alone do not establish passivity of the coupled system; this issue has prior analysis, including the Moreau-law discussion by Acary and Collins-Craft\cite{acary}. Test the actual energy increment using physical incoming velocities, not a restitution-shifted solver residual.

\section{Friction and the distinction between capacity and compliance}
The circular Coulomb impulse capacity is
\begin{equation}
j_n\ge0,\qquad \|\mathbf j_t\|\le\mu j_n.
\end{equation}
The current endpoint model seeks its tangential restitution target when capacity permits and limits the impulse when it does not. This capacity condition is not by itself a complete time-resolved friction law. In particular, a positive $e_t$ can represent stored shear energy returning during impact; a rigid, purely dissipative sliding law alone does not explain that return.

For a sphere with radius $R$, inertia $I=\alpha mR^2$, zero initial spin, and fixed plane, the uncapped tangential impulse is
\begin{equation}
\frac{j_t}{m}=-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}(1+e_t)u_t^-,\qquad
|\omega^+|=\frac{(1+e_t)|u_t^-|}{(1+\alpha)R}.
\end{equation}
These sphere formulas assume $\mathbf k=0$. When required capacity is exceeded, clamp this scalar impulse to $[-\mu j_n,\mu j_n]$; normal and tangential targets are uncoupled only in this special spherical geometry. Actual non-spherical inertia and lever arms produce coupling through $\mathbf W$.

A physically motivated extension would evolve normal indentation and tangential elastic displacement over a finite contact patch, integrate its force and moment, and account for elastic energy storage plus nonnegative dissipation. Restitution would then be an output or a constrained calibration target; adding endpoint restitution on top of an already dissipative compliant rebound risks double counting. A measured distributed moment is distinct from arbitrarily shifting a contact point until one outcome matches.


\subsection{Moment capacity is part of the contact law}
The translational Coulomb inequality alone does not specify $\mathbf k$. A finite patch can supply torsional and rolling moments, or moments from a distributed normal load. For example, simplified necessary capacity bounds may be written
\begin{equation}
|k_n|\le b_{\rm twist}j_n,\qquad
\|\mathbf k_t\|\le b_{\rm roll}j_n,
\end{equation}
where $k_n=\mathbf n^T\mathbf k$ and $\mathbf k_t=\mathbf k-k_n\mathbf n$. The physical coefficients $b_{\rm twist},b_{\rm roll}$ have units of length; they are distinct from the freely chosen coordinate lengths $\ell,\ell_c$. Their values and applicability must come from a measured or derived patch law. Separate force/moment bounds are generally not a complete jointly admissible patch-wrench law; integrating common tractions can couple the capacities. No new moment coefficient is fitted to the existing comparison outcomes.

The native comparisons retained here use force-only contacts. Their contact forces can change spin through lever-arm torque, but a separate free contact moment is absent. They therefore do not validate pure torsional spin resistance or a complete contact-wrench law. The corrected derivation identifies that missing mechanism explicitly; it does not silently change the historical comparison numbers.

\section{Efficient practical matrix calculations}
The scaled notation does not require constructing a dense global inverse. Store each body's mass, shape-frame inertia, attitude, and fixed reference length. For attitude matrix $\mathbf A$, rotate inertia once per pose: $\mathbf I_{\rm world}=\mathbf A\mathbf I_{\rm shape}\mathbf A^T$. Apply the inverse scaled mass to a vector by dividing its translation block by $m$ and solving a three-by-three inertia system for its rotation block, multiplied by $\ell^2$. In 2D the latter is scalar division by $I/\ell^2$. Use factorizations or the rotated inverse of a fixed shape inertia; avoid numerical inversion of a dense many-body matrix.

For a trial stacked wrench impulse $\boldsymbol\eta$, the matrix-free action $\mathbf W_w\boldsymbol\eta$ has three steps:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Accumulate each incident signed force and torque impulse through $\mathbf K_i^T\boldsymbol\eta_i$ into each body's scaled momentum increment.
\item Apply the body's inverse mass block once to its accumulated increment.
\item Gather the resulting signed contact velocity and scaled relative-spin increments through each $\mathbf K_i$.
\end{enumerate}
Shared bodies automatically generate the off-diagonal contact coupling. Coupled matrix-free contact evaluation is already taught in the SIGGRAPH 2022 contact tutorial\cite{siggraph}. This operation scales with bodies and contacts rather than the square of the global contact count. Assemble small island matrices when useful for factorization or diagnostics; preserve the full coupled law and acceptance gates. Independent pairwise impulses are not an equivalent substitute.

A practical prediction requires incoming translation and spin, mass and inertia, contact geometry and normal, and explicit $e_n,e_t,\mu$. For each active approaching contact, construct the restitution target and solve the simultaneous system with nonnegative normal impulse and the exact circular tangential capacity. Report whether each tangential target was realized or capacity-limited, outgoing velocities and spin, momentum balance, and work-corrected energy. Use the physical incoming contact velocity in the energy check. The native implementation has validated coefficient bounds, but not every large-scene trajectory refinement is qualified.

The accompanying \texttt{matrix\_tools.py} demonstrates scaled variables and matrix-free contact operations without changing production code. Its three-dimensional operations pass 100 seeded four-body, three-contact shared-chain comparisons between dense and matrix-free Delassus actions (maximum discrepancy $2.84\times10^{-14}$). Its native 2D and 3D prediction wrappers reproduce their retained demonstration trajectories exactly (\texttt{prediction-examples.json}). The planar restitution build remains isolated and has not qualified the full benchmark baseline. Each wrapper passes $e_n,e_t,\mu$ explicitly to the actual solver; the 3D illustrative pair assigns the square root of the pair coefficient to each body because Bullet multiplies body friction, while the 2D wrapper uses the Box2D square-root mixing convention. These checks do not establish a speedup or replace native simultaneous-solver verification. The demonstrated native force-only material/contact inputs are $e_n,e_t,\mu$. The full wrench matrices derived above are evaluated separately by \texttt{wrench\_impulse\_audit.py}: 100 random dynamic 3D pairs and 100 planar controls check direct force-plus-couple updates, reference-point shifts, coordinate scaling, angular balance and torque-inclusive energy/boundary work. These controls prescribe $\mathbf k$; they do not implement or validate a predictive native moment law. Rolling resistance, torsional resistance, independent contact moments, and compliant patch stiffness/damping would require additional measured laws and validation. They are not silently assumed present merely because their symbols could be added to a matrix.

\section{Mapping prediction deviations}
\label{sec:deviations}
For each measured comparison, collect a magnitude signature
\begin{equation}
\mathbf q=\begin{bmatrix}|v_n|\\|v_t|\\\ell|\omega|\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\phi=\arccos\frac{\mathbf q_{\rm measured}^T\mathbf q_{\rm predicted}}
{\|\mathbf q_{\rm measured}\|\,\|\mathbf q_{\rm predicted}\|},\qquad
\varepsilon=\frac{\|\mathbf q_{\rm predicted}\|}{\|\mathbf q_{\rm measured}\|}-1.
\end{equation}
Each case becomes one point $(\phi,\varepsilon)$: exact agreement is at the origin. The horizontal axis shows an incorrect mix of translation and rotation; the vertical axis shows too much or too little combined motion. It is the angle between observable signatures, not a spatial heading error. Colours identify collision types or declared contact regimes. Inputs need not be artificially separated, and different impacts can naturally share the same error coordinates.

The plotting policy takes $\ell$ equal to the reported radius, without fitting it to the errors. This converts spin into peripheral-speed units. Unlike physical predictions, numerical angles and norms of coordinate signatures depend on the chosen plotting metric. Only reported or explicitly reconstructed observables are used; unavailable vector signs and orientation are not invented. The main comparison below uses documented inputs; the historical fit-based map is separated in the appendix.

\section{What would establish a stronger model?}
First replace approximate shapes, inertias, and initial states with independently measured quantities wherever available. For rubber, compare a time-resolved compliant patch against the point model using independently characterized laws and parameters fixed before held-out impacts. Evaluate outgoing translation and rotation together, contact duration where measured, and a complete energy ledger. Translational energy alone need not be conserved: rotation, deformation, support motion, and dissipation exchange energy. Do not fit a separate force offset for every target outcome and call that an independent prediction.

For non-spherical rocks, contact location already changes the Jacobian and effective mass without changing restitution. Introduce a material map only if repeated experiments with measured geometry and state show a reproducible residual dependence that transfers to unseen contacts. The reference length $\Lref$ must never serve as an adjustable mechanism for improving agreement: it changes notation, not physics.
