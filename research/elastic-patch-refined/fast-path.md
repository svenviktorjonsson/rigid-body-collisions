# A conditional exact contact path and a resolved path for the same material

`research.elastic_impulse.resolve_impact` changes computational effort without changing the configured material. It attempts the exact matched-oscillator result only when stiffness, energy and instantaneous shared force/couple capacity prove its applicability. Otherwise it resolves the same sphere/plane constitutive law with adaptive integration, explicit elastic history, dissipation and energy/yield acceptance gates.

| Configured contact material and state | Computational method | Acceptance |
|---|---|---|
| Linear spring contact, matched normal/tangent/twist frequencies, zero damping and history, sufficient shared friction capacity | Exact impulse and contact trajectory | Analytic all-time capacity proof, complete energy check, compression budget |
| Weighted compression potential, unmatched spring frequencies, damping or yielding | Adaptive DOP853 material integration | Bounded RHS work, first lift-off, complete energy and shared yield checks; deformation bounded conservatively by total initial energy |
| Gravity during the contact, prior history, moving plane, several simultaneous contacts, arbitrary body or rolling couple | Not supported by this small dispatcher | Use the corresponding world/contact-history solver; do not invent a point impact result |

The separate `simulate` prototype supports fixed planes and gravity, including the tested repeated-floor example. The dispatcher deliberately accepts only one initially touching sphere against a fixed plane with zero gravity during that contact. The native many-body Coulomb engine is a different constitutive model; replacing an elastic bounce by an inelastic Coulomb bounce is **not** an accuracy/performance switch.

For the exact branch, the normal frequency is determined by the same normal stiffness and mass. Tangential stiffness uses the coupled translation/rotation effective mass, and torsional stiffness uses the sphere inertia. The three forces share a half-sine profile, so the peak scaled tangential force and independent torque divided by normal force remain constant throughout contact. The combined capacity condition is checked before any impulse is accepted. The independent angular impulse remains distinct from the angular momentum generated by the offset translational impulse.

Seven tests verify exact endpoints, the complete contact trajectory against refined material integration, energy and instantaneous force/couple capacity, rejection of unsupported assumptions, same-material slow/rapid impacts, the weighted-law fallback, and repeated back/forth motion on the same floor under gravity. The latter produces three alternating horizontal-velocity and tangent-axis-spin reversals. Gravity changes the finite contact duration slightly, leaving about 0.76 microjoules of explicitly accounted shear/slip/separation loss in that synthetic example. Its energy ledger stays within 10 nanjoules. This is a regression demonstration rather than a new separately preregistered trajectory benchmark.

The floor and ceiling oblique retracing examples in the report use different incoming spin relations. They do not establish a general chained oblique floor-to-ceiling retroreflector. The repeated vertical floor–ceiling example does verify the same vertical path and alternating normal-axis spin. All parameter values remain synthetic; no particular rubber compound has yet been calibrated or independently validated.

No generic speedup is claimed here. The exact branch avoids numerical substeps for its proven material assumptions, but comparisons of an endpoint map with a whole-world trajectory would not establish a fair engine performance result.
